People have thoughts, feelings, impulses, that they are taught are unacceptable. They are denied permission to experience their own emerging experiences, and so, learn to deny themselves permission to have or identify with those experiences. 

As this goes on people more and more stop identifying with the parts of themselves that they reject. 

Over time, this (ostensibly) functionally creates a whole other self composed of those parts of you that you have crushed down and denied- your "shadow self".

"Shadow work" is about reincorporating/reintegrating the parts of yourself that you've denied permission to exist within yourself. Parts Work, by any other name. 

Peoples' lust, or rage, or fear, or sexuality, or sentimentality can end up here as people demonize themselves for what they do or dodn't experience, and shadow work focuses on helping them find acceptance for those parts of themselves. 

Reincorporating the rejected parts helps bring them back into a sort of voluntary alignment with the person's desired behaviors/presentation, IIRC.


That’s a powerful connection to explore. Shadow work and intrusive thoughts intersect in meaningful ways—especially when it comes to self-acceptance and healing.

### Here’s how they relate:

#### 🌑 **1. Intrusive thoughts often arise from the shadow.**

Intrusive thoughts are usually unwanted, distressing, and feel “not me.” But often, they stem from the very parts of ourselves we’ve repressed—the shadow. They can reflect fears, shame, anger, or desires we’ve pushed down so hard that they burst through the cracks in unsettling ways.

#### 🪞 **2. Shadow work invites you to turn toward those thoughts—gently.**

Rather than reacting with fear, disgust, or shame, shadow work asks:

> _“What is this thought really trying to say?”_  
> _“What emotion, memory, or need might it be connected to?”_  
> _“Can I listen to it with curiosity instead of judgment?”_

This doesn’t mean acting on the thought—it means being willing to acknowledge it without labeling yourself as “bad” for having it.

#### 🫂 **3. It leads to self-compassion and reduces internal shame.**

The more you do shadow work, the more you realize: you’re not your thoughts. You’re the observer of them.  
This creates space for self-compassion:

> _“This thought is painful, but it doesn’t define me.”_  
> _“Maybe this part of me just wants to be heard or understood.”_

#### 🌱 **4. Integration quiets the chaos.**

When the shadow is seen and held with care, the mind doesn't have to scream through intrusive thoughts to get your attention. It's like calming a frightened child—once they feel safe, they stop yelling.
